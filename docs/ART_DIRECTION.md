# Project Gunner — Art Direction

This document defines the visual identity of Project Gunner. It governs the look, mood, shape language, and reference boundaries for characters, enemies, environments, weapons, props, VFX, lighting, and UI.

Before producing or modifying visual assets, also read:

- `docs/ART_BIBLE.md` for technical production standards.
- Any asset-specific brief under `docs/characters/`, `docs/enemies/`, `docs/environments/`, or another relevant documentation directory.
- The existing project files, import settings, materials, rigs, and scenes that the asset must support.

When this document conflicts with an asset-specific brief, the more specific brief governs that asset unless it violates the repository-wide originality or technical requirements.

---

## 1. Project Identity

Project Gunner is an original third-person cooperative action shooter built around:

- Heavy but responsive over-the-shoulder combat.
- Fast attachment to and movement along cover.
- Cooperative survival against an advancing alien horde.
- Environmental defenses, barricades, and movable objects.
- Readable combat spaces with clear threats and tactical choices.
- Local split-screen and LAN play.

The visual identity should feel grounded, tense, industrial, and battle-worn without becoming a copy of an existing military science-fiction or survival-horror property.

The game should look deliberately authored, not like a collection of unrelated marketplace assets.

---

## 2. Visual Pillars

Every major visual decision should support these pillars.

### 2.1 Grounded Stylized Realism

Use believable anatomy, construction, wear, lighting, and materials, but do not pursue expensive photorealism.

Favor:

- Realistic proportions with restrained heroic exaggeration.
- Strong silhouettes.
- Clear material separation.
- Controlled surface detail.
- Purposeful wear and damage.
- Details that remain visible from the gameplay camera.

Avoid:

- Excessive micro-detail that disappears during play.
- Film-quality complexity that slows production without improving gameplay.
- Unrealistically clean surfaces in a hostile setting.
- Visual noise caused by too many straps, pouches, panels, decals, and tiny attachments.

### 2.2 Readability Under Pressure

Players must understand characters, threats, cover, objectives, weapons, and interactable defenses quickly.

Use:

- Distinct silhouettes for each character and enemy role.
- Value and material contrast between adjacent forms.
- Clear weak points and attack states.
- Lighting that preserves combat readability.
- Color as reinforcement, not the only source of meaning.
- Large and medium forms before small decorative detail.

### 2.3 Functional Survival Design

Human equipment should appear adapted to the setting rather than selected only for visual coolness.

Every visible item should imply a function:

- Protection.
- Mobility.
- Weapon support.
- Communication.
- Repair.
- Medical response.
- Environmental defense.
- Carrying essential supplies.

Do not add equipment that has no plausible purpose merely to make a design appear more tactical.

### 2.4 Original Biological Threat

Alien enemies should feel like a coherent species or ecosystem rather than random monsters.

Their anatomy, movement, armor, sensory organs, and combat roles should share a repeatable design language.

They should appear capable of:

- Coordinated horde behavior.
- Traversing damaged industrial spaces.
- Pressuring defensive positions.
- Breaking, climbing over, passing through, or redirecting around obstacles.
- Evolving or specializing into distinct combat roles.

### 2.5 Achievable Production Scope

The art direction must be sustainable for a small project.

Prefer:

- Reusable skeletons.
- Shared material families.
- Modular environment kits.
- Trim sheets and atlases.
- Reusable decals.
- Limited but strong color palettes.
- A few polished asset families instead of many unfinished ones.

When choosing between more detail and a more readable, reusable, performant asset, choose readability, reuse, and performance.

## 3. Tone and Mood

The world should feel like a defensive line is failing, but not already hopeless.

Desired emotional qualities:

- Pressure.
- Isolation.
- Determination.
- Improvised resilience.
- Industrial decay.
- Uncertainty about the alien threat.
- Brief moments of safety surrounded by visible danger.

The tone is serious action with controlled horror elements.

Avoid:

- Constant darkness that makes navigation difficult.
- Excessive gore as the primary visual identity.
- Comedy props or exaggerated cartoon proportions unless specifically requested.
- Pure military realism.
- Gothic fantasy ornament.
- Clean utopian science fiction.
- Neon cyberpunk as the dominant language.

---

## 4. Reference and Originality Rules

Existing games, films, artwork, and real-world equipment may be used only as broad references.

Translate references into abstract qualities such as:

- Mood.
- Function.
- Proportion.
- Material behavior.
- Combat role.
- Visual hierarchy.
- Movement weight.
- Environmental storytelling.
- Camera readability.

Never reproduce a recognizable:

- Face.
- Hairstyle.
- Costume.
- Creature.
- Weapon silhouette.
- Logo.
- Insignia.
- Faction.
- UI composition.
- Environment layout.
- Signature animation.
- Story element.

When a prompt names an existing character or franchise, identify the general qualities being requested and create an original Project Gunner interpretation.

A final asset should not be mistaken for a character, prop, creature, or location from another property when viewed without explanation.

## 5. Human Character Direction

Human characters should look capable, weathered, and individual rather than superhuman or interchangeable.

### 5.1 Proportions

Use:

- Realistic adult proportions.
- Athletic builds appropriate for sprinting, crouching, vaulting, aiming, and carrying equipment.
- Slightly broadened shoulders when helpful for a strong third-person silhouette.
- Hands and footwear sized for clear animation and gameplay readability.
- Clothing volume that respects the underlying body.

Avoid:

- Bodybuilder proportions.
- Extremely narrow waists.
- Oversized armor.
- Fashion-model poses that undermine combat credibility.
- Identical body shapes across the entire cast.

### 5.2 Faces

Faces should include controlled asymmetry and distinguishing features.

Use combinations of:

- Different jaw and cheek structures.
- Nose and brow variation.
- Scars, healed injuries, fatigue, or weather exposure.
- Distinct hairlines and hair masses.
- Subtle stubble or grooming choices.
- Age-appropriate skin variation.

Avoid making every character conventionally perfect or relying on scars alone to create identity.

### 5.3 Clothing

Human clothing should use layered construction:

- Base layer.
- Utility or protective middle layer.
- Selective armor or reinforcement.
- Harness or attachment system.
- Durable trousers.
- Practical boots.
- Limited gloves or hand protection.

Clothing should communicate movement and survival, not ceremonial military status.

Use a restrained number of:

- Straps.
- Buckles.
- Pouches.
- Armor plates.
- Holsters.
- Cables.
- Decorative seams.

Each major character needs a readable front, side, and back silhouette.

### 5.4 Multiplayer Readability

Character A and Character B should remain distinguishable in split-screen and multiplayer through several combined signals:

- Different silhouette.
- Different upper-body shape.
- Different hair mass.
- Different jacket or armor construction.
- Different accent color.
- Different equipment arrangement.

Do not rely only on palette swaps.

## 6. Hero A Direction

Hero A is an original male survival-action protagonist.

### 6.1 Character Archetype

He should communicate:

- Competence under pressure.
- Alertness and intelligence.
- Experience without invincibility.
- Visible wear from prior encounters.
- A guarded but dependable personality.
- Strong third-person action-game presence.

A broad reference may be taken from the archetype of a capable, attractive, slightly weathered survival-horror protagonist. This is an archetype reference only.

Hero A must not be a reproduction of Leon S. Kennedy or any other existing character.

### 6.2 Approximate Appearance

- Adult male, approximately late twenties to mid-thirties.
- Athletic but not excessively muscular.
- Defined but imperfect facial structure.
- Dark brown or muted chestnut hair.
- Medium-length layered hair with an original asymmetrical swept shape.
- A hairstyle designed as a readable mass rather than individual expensive strands.
- Light facial wear such as subtle stubble, fatigue, or minor healed injuries.
- A narrow healed scar crossing one eyebrow and extending slightly toward the upper cheek.
- Natural facial asymmetry.

The scar should support recognition without becoming the entire character concept.

### 6.3 Originality Boundaries

Do not reproduce:

- Leon Kennedy's facial likeness.
- His exact hair silhouette or fringe.
- Any Resident Evil jacket.
- An RPD uniform.
- Resident Evil insignia.
- His exact holster layout.
- His exact colors or equipment arrangement.
- A costume immediately recognizable as Resident Evil cosplay.

### 6.4 Clothing Direction

Hero A should use an original layered field outfit built from elements such as:

- Fitted utility jacket or reinforced overshirt.
- Protective base layer.
- Selective lightweight armor.
- Durable field trousers.
- Practical boots.
- Partial hand protection.
- A limited harness or attachment system.
- A recognizable upper-back or shoulder feature visible from the normal camera.

The outfit should support:

- Sprinting.
- Crouching.
- Cover movement.
- Weapon aiming.
- Vaulting.
- Carrying defensive tools or supplies.

### 6.5 Suggested Palette

Use a controlled palette drawn from:

- Charcoal.
- Weathered black.
- Muted olive.
- Desaturated brown.
- Gunmetal.
- Dusty beige.
- One restrained character-specific accent.

Do not cover the entire character in similar dark values. The torso, arms, equipment, and legs must remain separable under gameplay lighting.

---

## 7. Hero B Direction

Hero B must be an equally deliberate original character, not a recolored version of Hero A.

Until a dedicated brief exists, preserve these constraints:

- Use a clearly different silhouette and equipment distribution.
- Use a different hair mass or head treatment.
- Use a distinct accent color.
- Avoid duplicating Hero A's jacket, scar placement, harness, or shoulder profile.
- Maintain compatible gameplay proportions and skeleton requirements where possible.
- Communicate a different background or role through functional design.

Create `docs/characters/HERO_B.md` before final production work on Hero B.

## 8. Alien Direction

Alien enemies must be original and visually connected through a shared biological language.

### 8.1 Shared Species Language

Establish repeatable rules for:

- Joint structure.
- Limb count and posture.
- Armor growth.
- Exposed tissue.
- Sensory organs.
- Mouth or feeding structures.
- Locomotion.
- Surface finish.
- Color transitions.
- Damage response.

Choose a limited set of shared motifs and repeat them across enemy roles.

Possible broad qualities include:

- Layered keratin or mineral-like armor.
- Flexible dark tissue between plates.
- Asymmetrical sensory clusters.
- Forward-driving posture.
- Limbs adapted for climbing or breaching.
- Bioluminescence used sparingly for communication or weak-point readability.

Do not combine every idea into every enemy.

### 8.2 Combat Role Readability

Enemy roles should be readable through body shape and movement before UI labels.

Examples:

- **Swarm attacker:** low center of gravity, forward posture, fast leg rhythm.
- **Breacher:** heavy front armor, reinforced limbs, large impact surfaces.
- **Ranged enemy:** elevated sensory or projectile anatomy, more exposed firing posture.
- **Disruptor:** unusual lateral silhouette, visible charge-up organs, movement that pressures cover.
- **Leader or coordinator:** distinctive posture, signaling organs, or controlled motion.

### 8.3 Originality Boundaries

Do not imitate recognizable creatures from:

- Alien.
- Predator.
- Gears of War.
- Dead Space.
- Halo.
- Starship Troopers.
- Resident Evil.
- Other established science-fiction or horror properties.

Avoid signature head shapes, jaw structures, armor patterns, or silhouettes strongly associated with those properties.

## 9. Environment Direction

Environments should support readable cover combat and environmental defense.

### 9.1 Setting Language

The initial visual language should favor:

- Industrial or infrastructure spaces.
- Functional architecture.
- Damaged defensive zones.
- Maintenance corridors.
- Loading areas.
- Utility rooms.
- Processing spaces.
- Storage and transit areas.
- Improvised barricades.
- Visible systems that explain how the space once worked.

The world should appear inhabited and used before the current disaster.

### 9.2 Shape Language

Human-built spaces:

- Rectilinear structure.
- Repeated modular bays.
- Clear support beams.
- Functional rails, doors, vents, and service routes.
- Large readable forms.
- Controlled diagonal braces for tension and movement.

Alien influence:

- Organic interruption of human geometry.
- Growth following seams, heat, moisture, damage, or power sources.
- Localized deformation rather than random decoration everywhere.
- Biological forms that affect traversal or defense.

### 9.3 Cover Readability

Cover should read clearly through:

- Height.
- Thickness.
- Material.
- Edge treatment.
- Lighting.
- Damage state.
- Placement.

Do not obscure gameplay-critical cover with decorative clutter.

High and low cover should be distinguishable from the gameplay camera.

### 9.4 Environmental Storytelling

Tell small, readable stories through:

- Failed defenses.
- Abandoned tools.
- Emergency repairs.
- Damaged doors.
- Partially deployed barriers.
- Power reroutes.
- Alien entry points.
- Civilian or worker traces without excessive exposition.

Environmental storytelling must not block movement, cover, navigation, or split-screen readability.

---

## 10. Defensive Objects and Interactables

Defensive objects are a core identity feature and should look practical, readable, and physically understandable.

Possible families:

- Fold-out barricades.
- Heavy movable crates.
- Locking shield panels.
- Welded scrap barriers.
- Expandable industrial gates.
- Portable power devices.
- Deployable hazard systems.
- Reinforcement tools.

Each object should clearly communicate:

- Whether it can be moved.
- Where it can be grabbed or deployed.
- Its active and inactive state.
- Its damage condition.
- The direction it blocks or affects.
- Whether aliens can climb, break, bypass, or attack it.

Do not use floating holographic interaction language as the only readability method. The physical design itself should explain the object's purpose.

---

## 11. Weapon and Equipment Direction

Weapons should appear engineered for the world and readable in third-person.

Favor:

- Strong primary silhouettes.
- Clear grip, stock, barrel, magazine, and functional separation.
- Industrial manufacturing logic.
- Moderate asymmetry where it improves originality.
- Materials that match the human faction's equipment.
- Visible wear at contact and moving areas.
- Attachment points that appear mechanically plausible.

Avoid:

- Direct imitation of recognizable real or fictional firearms.
- Needlessly complex rails and attachments.
- Extremely thin shapes that disappear during play.
- Huge fantasy weapons unless specifically justified.
- Decorative components with no apparent function.

Weapon families should share manufacturing cues without becoming identical.

---

## 12. Materials and Surface Language

Use materials to explain construction and function.

Human material families may include:

- Woven technical cloth.
- Reinforced synthetic fabric.
- Rubberized grip surfaces.
- Painted steel.
- Bare worn metal.
- Molded polymer.
- Leather-like utility material.
- Glass or display surfaces.
- Dust, oil, oxidation, and impact wear.

Alien material families may include:

- Hard armor growth.
- Flexible connective tissue.
- Wet or semi-gloss exposed surfaces.
- Dry membrane.
- Mineral deposits.
- Restrained emissive organs.

Wear should accumulate logically:

- Contact points.
- Exposed edges.
- Moving joints.
- Knees and elbows.
- Weapon grips.
- Boots.
- Barricade impact faces.
- Floor traffic routes.

Avoid uniform edge wear and random grunge covering every surface equally.

---

## 13. Color and Value

Use value structure first and color second.

### Human Spaces

Base families:

- Cool or neutral industrial grays.
- Desaturated greens and browns.
- Faded safety colors.
- Warm localized practical lighting.
- Controlled emergency red or amber.
- Limited faction accents.

### Alien Presence

Use a restricted alien accent family that contrasts with human construction.

The alien palette should not dominate every scene. Concentrate it around:

- Active growth.
- Weak points.
- Communication organs.
- Infestation sources.
- Major enemy attacks.
- Environmental corruption.

### Gameplay Communication

Reserve the clearest saturation and contrast for:

- Interactables.
- Objectives.
- Critical hazards.
- Enemy weak points.
- Player damage feedback.
- Team or character distinction.

Do not use color alone for critical information.

---

## 14. Lighting Direction

Lighting must support combat first and atmosphere second.

Use:

- Clear separation between playable routes and inaccessible background.
- Directional lighting that reveals cover edges.
- Local contrast around enemies and objectives.
- Practical light sources that make architectural sense.
- Controlled fog and volumetrics.
- Dark areas with readable silhouettes rather than crushed black values.
- Exposure stable enough for split-screen and fast camera movement.

Avoid:

- Constant flashing.
- Excessive bloom.
- Heavy chromatic aberration.
- Strong motion blur.
- Dense particles covering targets.
- Lighting that makes dark clothing disappear into the environment.

Test lighting from the actual over-the-shoulder camera and in split-screen.

---

## 15. VFX Direction

VFX should be fast, readable, and restrained.

Human VFX language:

- Angular or directional.
- Mechanical.
- Short-lived.
- Sparks, dust, fragments, pressure, heat, and controlled light.
- Clear distinction between muzzle, impact, armor hit, and objective feedback.

Alien VFX language:

- Organic timing.
- Pulsing, spreading, contracting, or splashing motion.
- Controlled fluidity.
- Distinct weak-point and attack charge cues.
- Limited emissive color.

Avoid effects that obscure aim or cover edges.

Gameplay meaning should be distinguished by shape, motion, timing, value, and sound—not only color.

---

## 16. UI Visual Direction

UI should feel connected to the game's industrial survival setting without becoming difficult to read.

Favor:

- Clear hierarchy.
- Bold but compact typography.
- Functional panels.
- Restrained framing.
- High contrast.
- Strong controller focus states.
- Limited animation.
- Physical or tactical visual cues used sparingly.

Avoid:

- Direct imitation of another game's HUD.
- Excessive distressed textures behind text.
- Tiny technical labels.
- Constant screen motion.
- Decorative panels that compete with gameplay.

Menus and HUD must remain legible at split-screen sizes.

---

## 17. Decision Rules for Agents

When visual direction is uncertain:

1. Inspect existing approved assets and documentation.
2. Preserve established working conventions.
3. Choose the option that improves gameplay readability.
4. Choose original interpretation over close imitation.
5. Choose reusable production methods over one-off complexity.
6. Choose performance appropriate for multiple characters and split-screen.
7. Record significant new conventions in the relevant documentation.
8. Continue without stopping for minor artistic approval unless the choice would materially redefine the project.

Do not claim an asset is complete until it is integrated and validated in Unreal Engine.

A concept image, Blender viewport render, model file, or successful export alone is not completion.
